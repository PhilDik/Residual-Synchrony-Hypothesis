Residual Synchrony Hypothesis

The Problem of Synchronous Emergence of Independent Ideas

Throughout the history of science and technology, there have been numerous cases in which different researchers independently arrived at the same or functionally equivalent discoveries and inventions within relatively short periods of time.

This phenomenon is usually explained by the shared state of scientific knowledge, technological readiness, communication between researchers, social and economic conditions, the number of people working on a particular problem, and the ordinary probability of independent coincidence.

However, a narrower question remains:

Do these known factors fully explain the temporal synchronization of independently emerging ideas?

It is possible to formulate a testable hypothesis that functionally similar ideas may arise among informationally independent individuals closer together in time than would be predicted by models accounting for known common causes.

This hypothesis does not require any mechanism to be assumed in advance. A possible unknown factor can provisionally be designated as X.

The purpose of the proposed research is not to demonstrate the existence of X, but to determine whether any statistically significant residual effect remains after known explanations have been progressively accounted for.

A model testing this hypothesis should account, as far as possible, for factors including:

* shared scientific literature and available knowledge;
* citations and possible information transfer between researchers;
* co-authorship networks and professional contacts;
* geographical and institutional proximity;
* technological readiness of the relevant field;
* changes in the number of researchers and publications over time;
* external events capable of independently directing attention toward the same problem;
* the probability of independent coincidence.

After controlling for these factors, the remaining residual temporal synchronization can be measured.

If the observed frequency and temporal proximity of independent discoveries can be reproduced by the control model, no additional unknown factor is required.

If, however, functionally equivalent ideas systematically arise among genuinely independent researchers closer together in time than predicted by the model, this would indicate the presence of an as-yet-unaccounted-for source of correlation. The nature of that source would then become a separate research question.

Quantum entanglement may be used only as a conceptual analogy for correlated states in the absence of an obvious direct connection. This hypothesis does not propose quantum entanglement as a mechanism for synchronization between human minds, nor does it assume any particular physical mechanism.

The central research question is therefore:

Does statistically significant residual synchronization exist in the independent emergence of functionally equivalent ideas after accounting for known informational, technological, social, and probabilistic causes?

If such a residual effect exists, its mechanism remains an open question.

Proposed Future Work

A future study could test this hypothesis using large-scale scientific publication and patent datasets.

Independent or near-independent instances of functionally equivalent discoveries could be identified, their earliest verifiable dates established, and their temporal separation measured.

An expected distribution could then be constructed while controlling for known sources of correlation.

The observed and expected temporal distributions could finally be compared to determine whether a residual concentration of independent discoveries exists at unusually short time intervals.

A positive result would establish an unexplained statistical correlation, not its mechanism.

A null result would indicate that the observed synchronization of independent discoveries can be adequately explained by known factors.
